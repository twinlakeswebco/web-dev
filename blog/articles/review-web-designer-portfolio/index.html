How to Review a Web Designer’s Portfolio Beyond Appearance

Open live projects, complete realistic customer tasks, ask what the designer contributed, and compare the work to your required scope.

Published November 13, 2026 · Casey Keown

A portfolio screenshot cannot show whether the contact form arrives, the mobile menu works, or the owner can update opening hours.

The direct answer

Open live projects, complete realistic customer tasks, ask what the designer contributed, and compare the work to your required scope.

Inspect the live experience

Use your phone and visit a service page, contact page, and any booking or purchase flow. Read the actual copy. Does it explain the business, or could the same words describe any company?

Avoid submitting fake orders or inquiries to another business. Ask the designer for a demo or recorded walkthrough when testing would create real work for a client.

Ask about constraints and contribution

Find out whether the designer handled the whole project or a specific part. Ask what the owner supplied, what platform was used, and why. Honest team credits are a positive sign.

Ask which constraint shaped the result. A simple site may be excellent work if it met a narrow budget and an owner’s editing needs. Complexity is not a quality score.

Look for a useful explanation of results

A designer can explain a better call path or shorter form without inventing revenue gains. If performance claims are presented, ask how they were measured, over what period, and what else changed.

Do not treat search rankings in one screenshot as permanent evidence. Location, device, personalization, and competition can affect results. Look for sound practices and clearly bounded claims.

Judge relevance to your project

A beautiful portfolio of restaurants may not demonstrate experience with an inventory system. A complicated application may not show an ability to write clear small-business service pages.

Ask for one comparable workflow and a handoff example. If the visual style differs from your preference, discuss whether the designer can adapt without copying another company’s identity.

Ask about an ordinary change

Have the designer show how the owner would add a service or replace a staff photo. A portfolio reveals the finished appearance, but an editing demonstration reveals how the business will operate the site. Notice whether the process requires a vendor for every small change, whether that arrangement was disclosed, and whether it fits your needs. Managed updates can be reasonable when their price and response expectations are clear.

Before you decide

  • Review live pages on mobile.
  • Ask who did each part.
  • Evaluate comparable functions.
  • Question unsupported performance claims.

Is a small portfolio a problem?

It limits the evidence, but does not automatically mean poor work. Use a demo, a bounded first phase, and explicit acceptance criteria.
