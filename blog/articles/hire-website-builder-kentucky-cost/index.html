Hiring Someone to Build a Website: A Kentucky Cost Breakdown

Your website quote should separate the initial build, recurring services, optional features, and future changes. A useful price has a written scope.

Published November 4, 2026 · Casey Keown

Two proposals can both say “five-page website” and buy you very different things. One includes the writing, photography preparation, form testing, and launch. The other gives you five empty layouts and a list of things to supply.

The direct answer

Your website quote should separate the initial build, recurring services, optional features, and future changes. A useful price has a written scope behind it.

I am Casey Keown, the Grayson County resident behind Twin Lakes Web Co. I work directly with business owners in Caneyville, Leitchfield, Clarkson, and across Kentucky. Here is how I would evaluate this purchase.

Price the work, then the pages

I start with what a visitor needs to accomplish. A contractor who needs calls and estimate requests has different requirements from a retailer taking payments or an organization accepting applications. Page count helps describe the project, but it does not describe the whole job.

Ask whether the quote includes content planning, writing, image preparation, design, development, mobile checks, accessibility work, search fundamentals, account setup, and launch. If an item is excluded, budget either money or your own time for it.

Use current local prices as examples

As of November 4, 2026, the Twin Lakes Web Co. contact page lists starting prices of $200 for The Dock custom link page, $700 for the Nolin Lake standard business website, and $1,434 for the Rough River business growth website. Those are different scopes, and final quotes may be lower or higher. They are my published starting points, not a Kentucky market average.

A small link page can be a sensible purchase when the immediate job is organizing existing destinations. A business that needs detailed service explanations and a dependable quote path should compare a broader scope. Read the current package details before assuming a price includes a particular feature.

Calculate a full year of ownership

List the domain renewal, hosting, business email, form service, booking system, paid plugins, maintenance, and support. Ask who bills for each item, which renewals change after an introductory period, and what happens if you cancel.

For a hypothetical comparison, a $900 build with $50 monthly service costs $1,500 in its first year before other expenses. A $1,400 build with $10 monthly hosting totals $1,520 on the same limited basis. Neither is automatically better. The included work and exit terms decide whether that small difference matters.

Reduce scope before reducing essentials

If the budget is tight, launch the services that earn the most useful inquiries first. Delay an elaborate gallery filter, custom animation, or an integration you do not yet use. Keep clear contact details, mobile usability, usable forms, secure account access, and ownership arrangements.

A repair company in Caneyville may need a service page and strong call path before it needs a blog. A shop shipping across Kentucky may need inventory and checkout work before decorative effects. The budget should follow the business process.

A practical Kentucky budget example

Suppose a new repair business needs a homepage, three service descriptions, project photos, and an estimate request. Before asking for a price, decide who writes the service text and whether customers upload photos. Those choices affect work more than whether the site has five or six pages. Ask for the launch version and a separate price for future booking. That gives you a usable comparison and keeps a speculative feature from consuming the initial budget.

Before you decide

  • Ask for separate build and recurring totals.
  • Name the pages and functions included.
  • Confirm who supplies copy and images.
  • Write down revisions, support, ownership, and exit terms.
  • Request a price for optional work before approving it.

Is the cheapest quote a bad choice?

No. A narrow, well-defined scope can cost less and still meet your needs. A vague low price becomes risky when essential work appears later as extras.
