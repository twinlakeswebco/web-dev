Farm Website Design for Kentucky Producers

A practical farm website guide for Kentucky producers selling food, livestock, services, subscriptions, events, or agritourism experiences.

Published October 4, 2026 · Casey Keown

A farm can have an excellent product and still make it hard for someone to buy.

The latest availability may exist only in a Facebook post. Pickup instructions may be buried in old messages. A customer may not know whether the farm sells individual cuts, whole or half animals, seasonal produce, breeding stock, hay, equipment services, event tickets, or farm stays. By the time the customer gets an answer, the sale may have moved somewhere else.

Farm website design in Kentucky should solve those practical problems. A useful farm website does not need to imitate a national food brand. It needs to explain what the farm offers, who can buy it, when it is available, where the customer goes, and what happens next.

I am Casey Keown, the Grayson County resident behind Twin Lakes Web Co. I build websites for local businesses and organizations, and farms deserve the same direct approach. The right site depends on how the operation actually earns money, not on how many features can be added.

Start With the Farm’s Real Sales Model

“Farm website” can describe very different businesses. A cattle producer selling freezer beef has a different customer journey from a produce farm offering weekly boxes. An agritourism operation has different needs from a hay producer, horse farm, greenhouse, apiary, sawmill, or farm-service contractor.

Before choosing pages or software, identify the transactions the website should support.

  • Direct retail orders
  • Deposits for livestock or bulk meat
  • Community supported agriculture subscriptions
  • Wholesale inquiries
  • Farm stand visits
  • Farmers market pickup
  • Agritourism reservations
  • Event registration
  • Breeding stock inquiries
  • Hay, equipment, or farm-service quotes

Choose the most important two or three. A website that tries to serve every possible farm customer from one crowded homepage usually serves none of them well.

The University of Kentucky Center for Crop Diversification notes in its online sales resources that marketing can be difficult to prioritize among all the work that has to happen on a farm. That is exactly why the website should reduce repetitive communication instead of creating another system to maintain.

Make Availability Clear Without Promising Too Much

Farm inventory changes. Produce is seasonal. Processing dates move. Weather affects harvests. Some products are reserved before they are ready. A useful website should communicate that reality without looking abandoned.

There are several workable approaches:

  • Show current inventory with quantities when records can be maintained accurately.
  • Label products as seasonal and collect availability notifications.
  • Use an inquiry or reservation form for products that require confirmation.
  • Explain expected harvest or pickup windows without guaranteeing a date the farm cannot control.
  • Mark sold-out products clearly instead of deleting every useful page.

Do not list everything as “in stock” if the customer still has to call and discover it is unavailable. Do not accept immediate payment for inventory that has not been allocated unless the terms explain exactly what is being purchased.

For a smaller operation, a current availability page and simple request form may work better than a full online store. For a farm with standardized products, reliable inventory, and regular fulfillment, ecommerce may save time. The technology should follow the operation.

Explain How Buying From the Farm Works

People who regularly buy direct from producers may understand deposits, hanging weights, pickup windows, CSA shares, or seasonal substitutions. A first-time customer may not.

Use plain language to explain:

  • What the customer receives
  • How pricing is calculated
  • Whether a deposit is refundable
  • When the balance is due
  • Where pickup or delivery happens
  • What identification or confirmation is required
  • Whether quantities or contents can change
  • How cancellations and missed pickups are handled

If a processor, market, or separate pickup partner is involved, describe each party’s role accurately. Do not make the customer guess who collects which payment or where the final product will be available.

This information can live on product, ordering, and frequently asked questions pages. My guide to essential small-business website pages can help organize those answers without turning the homepage into an instruction manual.

Build for Phones and Rural Connections

Customers may visit the website from a market, parking lot, field, campsite, or rural road. The site should remain useful when the connection is not perfect.

Large video backgrounds, oversized photographs, popups, and several tracking tools can delay the information a customer needs. Lead with clear text, compressed images, and simple navigation. Make phone numbers tappable. Keep ordering and inquiry forms short.

Test the site on an ordinary phone without relying on office Wi-Fi. Try to find today’s hours, current availability, directions, pickup instructions, and the order button. Then ask someone unfamiliar with the farm to do the same.

Good photography still matters. Customers want to see the real farm, people, animals, products, facilities, and experience when those images can be shared responsibly. My website photography guide explains how to plan original images while keeping pages fast.

Give Directions That Work Beyond a Street Address

Rural directions may require more context than a map pin. The mailing address may not be the customer entrance. A farm stand, event field, barn, or pickup point may use a different driveway.

A directions page can include:

  • The correct public address
  • A map link with a verified pin
  • The name or number on the entrance
  • Which driveway customers should use
  • Parking and trailer instructions
  • Accessibility information
  • Pickup hours and appointment requirements
  • A phone number for day-of questions

Do not publish a private residence or production area as a public customer entrance unless visitors are actually supposed to go there. If visits are appointment-only, say that clearly. Accurate directions prevent unsafe turnarounds, blocked work areas, and frustrated customers.

For farms serving a broad area without receiving customers at the farm, use an honest service-area approach. The service-area pages guide explains how to describe real coverage without creating copied town pages.

Separate Direct Customers From Wholesale Buyers

A restaurant, retailer, school, distributor, or institutional buyer needs different information from a household shopper.

Wholesale buyers may look for product specifications, volume, season, packaging, delivery area, lead time, certifications, insurance information, and the right contact person. Retail customers may care more about price, pickup, preparation, and availability.

Give each audience a clear path. A wholesale page does not need to publish confidential pricing or promise capacity. It can explain the products and process, identify the information needed for an inquiry, and route the request to the correct person.

The USDA Agricultural Marketing Service local food directories are intended to help consumers and wholesale buyers identify nearby suppliers. An eligible producer can review the directory types and listing requirements, but a directory profile should point back to a website that answers the buyer’s next questions.

Treat Agritourism as a Visit, Not Just a Product

U-pick operations, farm tours, pumpkin patches, workshops, dinners, weddings, stays, and seasonal events need visitor information that product pages do not cover.

Publish the date and operating hours, admission details, reservation rules, weather plan, age guidance, clothing or footwear recommendations, accessibility information, parking, animal rules, photography policy, and refund terms that apply.

If capacity is limited, an online booking system can reduce back-and-forth messages. It should prevent overbooking, send confirmations, explain changes, and work on a phone. If the farm is not ready to maintain online availability, a request form may be safer than instant booking.

Keep one permanent page for a recurring event when possible. Update the date, schedule, and registration link instead of creating a disconnected page every year. After the event, remove expired buttons and explain when new information will be posted.

Use Kentucky and Agricultural Directories Honestly

A farm’s website and directory profiles should agree on its name, phone number, website, public address or service area, and customer hours.

Kentucky Proud is the Commonwealth’s official agricultural marketing brand, and the Kentucky Department of Agriculture provides membership information and applications for eligible operations. Do not display a program logo or membership claim unless the farm is actually authorized to use it.

The USDA also maintains directories for categories such as farmers markets, on-farm markets, community supported agriculture programs, and food hubs. Review the definitions before submitting. Choose the category that describes the operation rather than the one that seems likely to attract the most views.

These profiles support discovery, but they do not replace the farm’s own site. The website is where the producer can explain the full process, maintain current details, collect inquiries, and build an audience it can reach directly. My guide to local citations for small businesses covers listing ownership and cleanup in more detail.

Build an Email List the Farm Controls

Social media is useful for showing daily work and announcing quick changes, but posts move out of view. An email list gives customers a direct way to receive availability notices, preorder dates, market schedules, CSA openings, or event announcements.

Ask only for the information needed. Explain what subscribers will receive and how often. Use a real email service with unsubscribe tools instead of sending bulk messages from a personal inbox.

Place signup forms where the reason is clear. Someone reading a sold-out beef page may want processing-date alerts. A visitor viewing an event page may want next season’s announcement. A general “join our newsletter” box is less useful than a specific promise.

Keep Accounts and Content Under the Farm’s Control

The farm should control its domain, website, payment account, email list, analytics, business profiles, product photographs, and customer data. A web designer or marketing provider can have appropriate access without becoming the only owner.

Document renewal dates, administrators, recovery contacts, and recurring costs. Decide who updates inventory, hours, events, and pickup details. A website becomes unreliable when everyone assumes someone else is maintaining it.

Do not collect more customer information than the transaction needs. Protect account access with multifactor authentication where available. Limit staff permissions to their responsibilities, and remove access when a role changes.

Measure Useful Farm Website Actions

Traffic alone does not show whether the website helps the operation. Track actions tied to the sales model:

  • Completed orders or deposits
  • Availability requests
  • CSA applications
  • Wholesale inquiries
  • Event reservations
  • Directions and phone clicks
  • Email signups
  • Repeat orders

Ask customers how they found the farm. Review which pages begin useful inquiries and which questions still arrive repeatedly by message. Those repeated questions identify content the website should improve.

Build the Website the Operation Can Maintain

A Kentucky farm does not need every feature on day one. It needs accurate product information, a clear buying process, dependable directions, fast mobile pages, real photography, and one obvious next step.

Start with the work the farm can support. Add ecommerce, subscriptions, wholesale tools, event booking, or customer accounts when they reduce labor and fit the real process. A smaller, current website will serve customers better than an elaborate system nobody has time to update.
