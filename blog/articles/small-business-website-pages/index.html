7 Essential Website Pages for Grayson County Businesses

Learn which pages a Grayson County small business website needs, what each page should accomplish, and when a simple site is enough.

Published September 17, 2026 · Casey Keown

A business website can have plenty of words and still leave customers unsure about what to do next.

The homepage says the company provides quality service. The about page repeats the same promise. The services page lists a few broad categories. Then the contact page asks someone to submit a form without explaining what happens afterward.

That is not really a page-count problem. It is a planning problem.

The right small business website pages should guide a visitor from a question to a confident next step. A contractor's site may need detailed service and project pages. A restaurant needs current menu, hours, and location information. A cabin or marina site needs accurate amenities, policies, directions, and inquiry or booking options.

I am Casey Keown, the Grayson County resident behind Twin Lakes Web Co. Here is how I would structure a practical website for a business in Leitchfield, Clarkson, Caneyville, around Nolin or Rough River Lake, or elsewhere in the county.

Start With Customer Questions, Not a Page Quota

There is no universal number of pages that every business needs. A focused one-page site can be appropriate for a new business with one clear offer. An established company with several services, locations, or customer types will usually need more room.

Before naming pages, write down what a visitor needs to know:

  • What does this business provide?
  • Is this service meant for me?
  • Does the business serve my location?
  • Can I see real work, products, people, or property?
  • What does the process involve?
  • What should I expect before I call or request a quote?
  • How do I take the next step?

Each important question needs an obvious answer. Some answers belong together. Others deserve a separate page because the customer needs detail or because people search for that specific service.

Google's SEO Starter Guide recommends organizing a site logically and creating content that is useful, easy to read, and made for people. A clear structure helps visitors explore the site and helps search engines understand how its subjects relate.

1. A Homepage That Explains the Business Quickly

The homepage should orient a visitor, not tell the company's entire history.

Near the top, it should identify the business, the main service or product, the general service area, and the next action. A visitor should not have to study a rotating slideshow or decode a clever slogan to understand the offer.

A useful homepage usually includes:

  • A direct headline
  • A short explanation of the main offer
  • Primary service or product links
  • A reason to trust the business
  • Real photographs or relevant visuals
  • A clear call, booking, visit, or quote action
  • The main service area when location matters

For example, a Leitchfield contractor might lead with the type of work completed and the counties served. A Clarkson restaurant should make hours, directions, and menu information easy to reach. A lake-area rental should help a visitor reach availability, capacity, amenities, and policies without searching through several menus.

The homepage should link people into the site. It should not force every subject onto one very long page.

2. Separate Pages for Important Services or Products

A page labeled “Services” is useful as an overview, but a short list of service names rarely answers enough questions.

When a service is important to the business, give it a focused page. Explain who it is for, what is included, what the process looks like, which problems it solves, and how someone gets started. Include real examples and appropriate service-area information.

Separate service pages also give search engines a clear page to evaluate for a specific topic. A single paragraph that mentions ten unrelated services cannot provide the same depth.

That does not mean creating dozens of thin pages. Do not copy the same text and change one service or town name. Combine closely related offers when customers view them as one decision. Split them when the work, audience, questions, or next step is meaningfully different.

A contractor may need individual pages for major lines of work. A repair company may separate residential and commercial services. A marina may separate slips, rentals, fuel, and repair if each has enough information to help a visitor. 3. An About Page That Builds Real Trust

The about page is not a place for vague claims about excellence and passion. It should help a customer understand who is behind the business and why the business is qualified to do the work.

Useful about-page content may include:

  • The owner or leadership team
  • The company's history
  • Relevant experience, licenses, or certifications
  • The reason the business exists
  • The people customers will work with
  • The community or service area the business actually serves
  • A straightforward next step

Use real photographs when possible. Name people and roles only with their permission, and keep the page current when the team changes.

Local context should be truthful and specific. A Grayson County business can explain its genuine connection to Leitchfield, Clarkson, Caneyville, Nolin Lake, Rough River Lake, or surrounding communities without inventing a storefront or stuffing town names into every paragraph.

4. A Work, Portfolio, Menu, or Results Page

Customers need evidence. The right proof page depends on the business.

A contractor can show completed projects. A restaurant can publish a readable current menu with original food photography. A hauling or service company can explain equipment, capabilities, and representative work. A nonprofit can show programs, events, and community impact without exaggerating results. A cabin or venue can show the property, amenities, layout, and guest expectations.

Whenever possible, add context instead of posting an unexplained image gallery. Describe the customer's need, the work completed, the relevant decisions, and the finished result. Only publish measurable outcomes when the business has evidence for them.

Testimonials and reviews can support this page when they are authentic and used with permission. Do not edit a customer's statement into a claim the person did not make.

5. A Frequently Asked Questions Page

A useful FAQ page reduces uncertainty before a customer calls. It should answer real questions, not manufacture obvious questions just to repeat sales language.

Good FAQ subjects may include:

  • Service areas
  • Scheduling and response expectations
  • Estimates or consultation steps
  • What the customer should prepare
  • Payment methods or deposit policies
  • Cancellations and rescheduling
  • Delivery, pickup, parking, or arrival details
  • Seasonal availability
  • Warranty or support boundaries

Some answers belong on the relevant service page too. That is fine. The FAQ can summarize common issues while the detailed page handles the full explanation.

Avoid promising exact timelines, availability, pricing, or results unless the business can consistently honor them. When an answer changes by project, explain the factors that affect it and tell the visitor how to get a specific answer.

6. A Contact Page Designed Around the Next Step

The contact page should remove friction, not create another obstacle.

Include the contact methods the business actually monitors. That may be a phone number, email address, short form, booking link, public address, or directions. If customers cannot visit the business, do not publish a private home address or imply that it is a walk-in office.

Tell the visitor what information will help and what happens after submission. A quote form for a contractor may ask about the project type, location, timing, and preferred contact method. A general contact form should not demand a long project history before someone can ask a basic question.

Keep forms short enough to complete on a phone. Confirm that submissions reach the correct inbox, show a clear success message, and provide another contact option when the request is urgent.

The mobile-friendly website checklist covers clickable phone numbers, simple forms, readable text, and other phone-first details that affect local customers.

7. Policy and Legal Pages That Match the Site

Legal and policy needs depend on what the website does. A basic brochure site, online store, appointment system, newsletter, and customer portal do not collect or process the same information.

Possible pages include:

  • Privacy policy
  • Terms and conditions
  • Return, refund, or cancellation policy
  • Shipping or delivery policy
  • Accessibility statement
  • Warranty information
  • Required industry disclosures

These pages should match actual business practices and website tools. Copying another company's policy can create contradictions. A privacy policy should not claim the site collects nothing if forms, analytics, payments, scheduling tools, or embedded services collect information.

The Federal Trade Commission's small-business guidance on hiring a web host recommends asking about security protections and whether a provider uses TLS to help protect customer information in transit. For legal requirements, contracts, regulated services, or customer-data questions, get advice from a qualified attorney rather than treating a template as legal review.

When a Blog or Resource Section Makes Sense

A blog is not one of the seven required pages for every business. It is valuable when the company can publish genuinely useful information that supports customer decisions.

A contractor might explain project preparation. A lake business can publish accurate seasonal instructions or event details. A professional service can answer complicated questions that do not fit on a sales page.

Do not start a blog just to fill a calendar. Google says its systems prioritize helpful, reliable, people-first content. Its current guidance for generative AI search also emphasizes unique, valuable content instead of special AI-search tricks or commodity pages.

A Simple Structure Is Often Better

More pages do not automatically create a better website. Every page should have a clear purpose, accurate content, an obvious relationship to the rest of the site, and a useful next step.

For many Grayson County businesses, a strong starting structure is home, services, individual service pages, about, work or proof, FAQ, contact, and the policies the operation actually needs. A smaller business can combine some of those subjects. A more complex operation may expand them.

The goal is not to reach a certain page count. It is to make sure a customer can understand the offer, verify the business, answer practical questions, and act without confusion.
