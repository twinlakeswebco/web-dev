# How Much Does a Small Business Website Cost?

Ask how much a small business website costs and you will hear answers ranging from “almost free” to “more than a new truck.” Both answers can be true. The problem is that they usually describe completely different products.

A do-it-yourself page built from a template is not the same purchase as a professionally planned website with original copy, local SEO, online scheduling, accessibility work, and ongoing support. Before you compare prices, you need to compare scope, ownership, and what the site is expected to accomplish.

For most small businesses, the useful question is not, “What is the cheapest way to get online?” It is, “What should I budget for a website that helps customers understand, trust, and contact my business?”

This guide gives you a realistic way to answer that question without pretending there is one universal price.

## The Short Answer: Website Costs Vary by Approach

Here is a practical planning framework for a typical small-business website in 2026. These are budgeting ranges, not quotes or promises:

- **DIY website builder:** roughly $200 to $1,000 during the first year, plus your own time.
- **Freelance designer or developer:** commonly $1,000 to $8,000 for a straightforward business site.
- **Specialized local studio or boutique team:** often $3,000 to $15,000 when strategy, custom design, content, and SEO are included.
- **Full-service agency:** often $10,000 to $35,000 or more.
- **E-commerce or custom web application:** frequently $5,000 to $50,000 or more, depending on products, integrations, accounts, payments, and automation.

Published estimates vary just as widely. Elementor’s 2026 guide places professional freelance WordPress projects around $2,000 to $8,000 and agency projects around $10,000 to $35,000. Meanwhile, one Grayson County competitor publicly advertises custom sites beginning at $900. That contrast shows why a price without a scope tells you very little. ([Elementor’s 2026 cost guide](https://elementor.com/blog/how-much-does-a-small-business-website-cost/); [Studio 925 pricing](https://studio925.design/website-cost))

The correct range for your business depends on what you need the website to do.

## What Actually Changes the Price?

Most website estimates are built from the same major ingredients. Understanding them makes it much easier to compare proposals.

### 1. The Number and Purpose of the Pages

A five-page website for a local service business is usually less expensive than a 25-page site covering multiple services, cities, staff members, and resources.

Page count is not the only issue. A carefully researched service page designed to rank in Google and answer customer objections requires more work than a page containing a few sentences and a stock photo.

A basic small-business site often includes:

- Home
- About
- Services
- Reviews or projects
- Contact

A stronger lead-generation site may also need a separate page for every major service, service area, customer type, or frequently asked question. Those pages can improve clarity and search visibility, but they require planning, writing, and development.

### 2. Template Design Versus Custom Design

Templates reduce the initial design time. That can make sense for a new business with a limited budget and simple needs. The tradeoff is that your content must often fit the template instead of the design being shaped around your customers.

Custom design costs more because decisions are made specifically for your business: page hierarchy, navigation, calls to action, typography, mobile behavior, imagery, and brand presentation.

“Custom” should also mean more than changing a template’s colors. Ask the provider what is actually being created and what is being reused.

### 3. Content, Photography, and Brand Assets

Someone has to write the words, organize the services, choose the photos, and explain why a customer should call you.

Some estimates assume you will provide finished copy and properly sized images. Others include interviews, copywriting, photo selection, editing, logo cleanup, or original photography. A low estimate can become expensive if you later discover that none of the content work was included.

If you are supplying content, ask for a checklist and deadline. Delayed content is one of the most common reasons a website project stalls.

### 4. Features and Integrations

A contact form is relatively simple. A customer portal, searchable inventory, booking system, online store, quote calculator, membership area, or connection to existing business software is not.

Features affect both the original build and the long-term cost. For example, an online store may require:

- Product setup and variations
- Payment processing
- Shipping or pickup rules
- Tax configuration
- Customer emails
- Inventory management
- Refund and privacy policies
- Ongoing platform or application fees

Write down the business process you want to improve before asking for a quote. “I need customers to schedule and pay a deposit” is much more useful than “I need a modern website.”

### 5. SEO, Mobile Performance, and Accessibility

Search engine optimization is not a switch that gets flipped at launch. The foundation includes clear page structure, useful content, descriptive titles, crawlable links, fast assets, local signals, and accurate business information.

Mobile work is especially important because Google uses the mobile version of a site for indexing and ranking. Google also recommends good Core Web Vitals, which measure loading performance, responsiveness, and visual stability. ([Google’s mobile-first indexing guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/mobile/mobile-sites-mobile-first-indexing); [Google’s Core Web Vitals guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/core-web-vitals))

Ask whether the price includes:

- Mobile and tablet testing
- Image optimization
- Page titles and meta descriptions
- LocalBusiness or Organization schema where appropriate
- Search Console and analytics setup
- Redirects from old URLs
- Basic accessibility checks

If the proposal simply says “SEO included,” ask what that means.

## The Build Price Is Not the Total Cost

Business owners often focus on the initial invoice and overlook the cost of keeping the website useful.

### Domain Name

Your domain is the address customers type into the browser. It is normally renewed annually. Some platforms advertise a free first year with an annual plan, but the renewal is a separate future expense. Wix, for example, explains that its first-year domain voucher does not cover renewals. ([Wix plan details](https://www.wix.com/plans))

Make sure the domain is registered in an account your business controls. A web designer can help manage it without becoming its hidden owner.

### Hosting or Platform Subscription

Every website needs somewhere to live. With a hosted builder, hosting is bundled into the monthly or annual subscription. A custom-coded or WordPress site may use a separate hosting provider.

Website-builder pricing can range from a modest monthly charge to substantially more for advanced commerce features. Wix’s May 2026 overview describes builder plans ranging from free options to approximately $150–$200 per month, depending on features. ([Wix website cost guide](https://www.wix.com/blog/how-much-does-a-website-cost))

Do not compare subscription prices without comparing what is included: backups, security, support, email, storage, applications, transaction fees, and the ability to move the site later.

### Maintenance and Updates

A simple static website may need very little technical maintenance. A site using a content management system, theme, and plugins needs regular updates, backups, compatibility checks, and security attention.

Content also changes. Employees leave, prices change, services expand, and photos get outdated. Decide who will make those changes and whether support is included, billed hourly, or covered by a maintenance plan.

### Marketing Is Separate From Construction

Launching a website does not automatically create an audience. Depending on your goals, the next phase may include local SEO, new service pages, Google Business Profile work, email marketing, paid advertising, or regular articles.

Think of the website as the place your marketing sends people, not the entire marketing plan by itself.

## Three Realistic Small-Business Scenarios

The following examples show why two businesses can receive very different quotes.

### A New Local Contractor

A contractor serving one or two Kentucky counties may need five to eight pages, project photos, a quote form, review highlights, and pages explaining the most profitable services.

The main value is not a complicated feature. It is making the company look trustworthy and giving Google and customers enough information to understand the work and service area.

### An Established Restaurant

A restaurant may need menus that work well on phones, location and hours, online ordering links, photography, event information, and easy updates. The build can remain straightforward if ordering is handled by an existing service.

Building a completely custom ordering platform would move the project into a different cost category.

### A Business Replacing Spreadsheets

A company that needs customer accounts, case tracking, document generation, or a custom quoting workflow does not merely need a website. It needs a web application.

The planning must cover permissions, data, security, testing, backups, and ongoing development. Comparing that project to a five-page brochure site would be like comparing a filing cabinet to an entire office.

## How to Set Your Website Budget

Use this five-step process before requesting estimates.

1. **Define the result.** Decide whether the priority is phone calls, appointments, sales, applications, donations, or reducing repetitive work.
2. **List the required pages and features.** Separate genuine requirements from ideas that could wait until a second phase.
3. **Identify what you can provide.** Be honest about whether you have finished copy, usable photographs, branding, and time to manage the project.
4. **Choose an ownership model.** Decide whether you prefer a lower initial cost with a continuing subscription or a larger one-time build with separate hosting and support.
5. **Reserve money for improvement.** A practical website gets better after launch as you learn what customers search for and where they hesitate.

For a local service business, it is often wiser to launch a focused site that clearly explains the company than to spend heavily on features customers do not need.

## What a Website Proposal Should Tell You

Do not accept a single dollar amount with no explanation. A useful proposal should identify:

- The pages and features included
- Who supplies or writes the content
- How many revision rounds are included
- Expected project milestones
- Domain and hosting responsibilities
- Mobile, SEO, and analytics setup
- Ownership of the design, code, content, and accounts
- Maintenance or support after launch
- Additional costs that are not included

Also ask what would cause the price to change. Clear boundaries protect both the business owner and the developer.

Be cautious with promises of guaranteed Google rankings, unexplained recurring charges, pressure to buy immediately, or arrangements where the provider controls every account. Price matters, but clarity and ownership matter too.

## So, How Much Should You Spend?

A small business website should cost enough to solve the right problem, without paying for complexity that does not help the business.

If you mainly need a professional online presence, a focused site may be enough. If you depend on local search, you may need deeper service and location content. If customers must buy, schedule, upload documents, or manage accounts, expect the scope and budget to grow.

Start with the outcome, not the number of animations or the name of the platform. Then compare proposals line by line. The cheapest quote can be a good value when the scope is honest. A higher quote can also be a good value when it includes strategy, content, ownership, performance, and dependable support.

The goal is not simply to own a website. It is to own a useful business asset that customers can find, understand, and trust.
