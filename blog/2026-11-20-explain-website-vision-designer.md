# How to Explain Your Website Vision to a Designer

“Clean and modern” means different things to different people. A designer needs decisions that can be tested.

## The direct answer

Translate visual preferences into business goals, customer tasks, content priorities, and a few annotated examples. Explain why each example helps.

## Start with the customer’s situation

Describe who arrives, what they need, and the next action. A homeowner requesting a repair has different priorities from someone comparing a large planned project.

Write the three questions visitors ask most often. Those answers help determine the homepage, service pages, navigation, and contact options.

## Annotate references

Choose a few examples and explain the specific feature you like: readable service categories, a useful project gallery, a clear booking path, or restrained typography.

Also identify what you dislike. References should guide a new design, not authorize copying another company’s logo, writing, photos, or page artwork.

## Separate requirements from preferences

A form that routes to the correct office is a requirement. A particular animation is a preference unless it performs a defined job. Make that distinction visible in the brief.

Include constraints such as existing brand colors, editing needs, accessibility, photo availability, and the launch date. A designer can work around constraints more effectively when they arrive early.

## Review with specific feedback

Say “the main service is missing above the fold” or “the button looks like a label” rather than “something feels off.” Explain the consequence for the customer.

When several people review the site, consolidate their notes. Contradictory instructions create work without creating a better result.

## Translate a vague preference

Instead of asking for a more premium feel, explain that customers compare substantial projects and need to see workmanship, understand the process, and find the owner. The designer can respond with stronger project photography, readable case studies, careful typography, and a clear introduction. Those choices can be evaluated. Repeatedly swapping colors without discussing the customer’s uncertainty may never solve the underlying problem.

## Before you decide

- Name audience and primary task.
- Annotate references.
- Separate requirements from preferences.
- Consolidate feedback.

## Do I need to know design terminology?

No. Plain descriptions of the customer problem are enough. The designer should translate them into design decisions.
